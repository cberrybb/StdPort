## Advanced beginners only

> Knowing the rules is useful. Knowing when to ignore them is more interesting.

This project explores typography through a series of deliberately constrained experiments. The aim was to test how hierarchy, scale, spacing and alignment affect the way information is read — and how quickly a perfectly sensible layout can become much more interesting once the rules start being pushed.

Rather than treating type as decoration, the work focuses on typography as the structure of the page: what gets noticed first, what gets read next, and what can be made awkward on purpose without becoming unusable.

### Finding the system

Early studies looked at simple relationships between type size, weight, spacing and position. Repetition helped establish a visual system before introducing more disruptive changes.

The process moved between controlled layouts and more expressive compositions, keeping enough consistency that the work still felt like part of the same family.

### Breaking it carefully

Once the basic hierarchy was working, the experiments became less polite. Scale shifts, cropping, tighter spacing and unexpected alignments were used to test how far the system could bend before it stopped communicating clearly.

The strongest outcomes were not necessarily the cleanest. They were the ones where the typography still carried the information while also becoming the main visual voice of the piece.

### What survived

The final work keeps a balance between structure and disruption: enough order to remain readable, but enough tension to avoid looking like a typography exercise that behaved itself.

#### Notes

- Hierarchy matters more than decoration.
- Consistency gives you something useful to break.
- White space is part of the typography.
- If everything is shouting, nothing is.
